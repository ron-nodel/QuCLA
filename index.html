Predicting how semiconductor quantum-dot devices behave, before they are built.

MaSQE (Modeling and Simulation for Quantum Exploration) is our device-modeling suite for gate-defined semiconductor quantum dots. It solves the multi-electron Schrödinger equation by full configuration interaction, self-consistently with the electrostatic potential of a realistic device geometry, on real-space grids across multiple domains and models (MDMM). The result is the energy spectrum of coupled quantum-dot systems in silicon and germanium, accurate enough to guide device design, extract effective parameters such as charging energies, lever arms, and exchange couplings, and compare directly with experiment.

The simulation suite everything else is built on.

MaSQE: Schrödinger–Poisson and FCI at device scale

Modeling and Simulation for Quantum Exploration (MaSQE) is the group's device-modeling suite for gate-defined semiconductor quantum dots. Its multi-domain, multi-model (MDMM) framework computes the self-consistent Schrödinger–Poisson electrostatics of realistic layered devices, and a full configuration interaction (FCI) solver on real-space grids with a continuously adapted orthonormal orbital basis gives high-precision ground and excited few-electron states. The code was developed with HRL Laboratories and has been used to model exchange-only triple-dot qubits, scanning-gate microscopy of double dots, and multielectron dots.

Fast, simulation-derived models of real devices, and locating the defects inside them.

Charge-stability digital twins for Si/SiGe devices

Operating spin qubits requires tuning the electron occupancy under each gate, traditionally mapped with charge stability diagrams of occupancy versus two gate voltages. Simulating these pixel by pixel from full device models is accurate but computationally prohibitive for design iteration. Using the MDMM framework, an automated tuning procedure identifies a small set of gate-voltage reference points corresponding to specific charge states, and FCI calculations at those points give charging energies, lever arms, and interdot Coulomb interactions that parameterize a Hubbard model directly. The Hubbard model then reconstructs the full diagram efficiently, enabling rapid pre-fabrication optimization of gate geometry and voltage ranges. The method is validated against bias-sweep simulations and experimental data from an Intel Tunnel Falls Si/SiGe device. The framework also identifies experimentally accessible Hubbard parameters for analog quantum simulation and is being extended to non-constant lever arms and tunnel couplings near the tie-bar region used for Pauli-blockade readout.

With Hong-Wen Jiang and Jason Petta (UCLA)

Locating two-level fluctuators with device simulations

Charge noise in spin qubit devices is often dominated by individual two-level fluctuators (TLFs), charge traps that switch between two states and shift the dot energies. Fundamental limits on gate fidelities are likely to sit at the materials and device level, so knowing where these fluctuators are is a first step toward removing them. Combining high-precision simulations of realistic devices with experimental data, we developed a procedure that triangulates the spatial location of individual charge fluctuators from their signatures on different gates. The procedure is general and can be applied to data from any spin qubit device. [Add the TLF paper and a figure.]

Engineering, mapping, and exploiting the valley degree of freedom in Si/SiGe.

Valley splitting and heterostructure design

Electrons in strained silicon quantum wells carry a low-lying valley degeneracy whose splitting is set by atomic-scale alloy disorder at the Si/SiGe interface. Small or random valley splittings are therefore a primary obstacle to reliable spin-qubit operation, initialization, and readout. This project shows that simple heterostructure engineering can guarantee a large valley splitting. Using one-dimensional tight-binding device-scale simulations (2k0 theory) that incorporate atomistic alloy disorder, we identified an unorthodox stack, a narrow quantum well combined with a small Ge spike and a pure-Ge cap, that consistently yields valley splittings of 1 to 5 meV across disorder realizations, corroborated by density functional theory. If realized experimentally, this design effectively removes valley splitting as an existential bottleneck for large-scale SiGe quantum processors. The germanium-spike idea builds on earlier experimental work by group alumni.

With Efrain Martinez and Peihong Zhang (UCLA), and Vince Lordi and Joel Varley (Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory)

Mapping valley splitting with a double quantum well and a scanning gate

Spatial variations in valley splitting are a challenge for high-fidelity Si/SiGe quantum dot qubits, because low-lying valley states lead to leakage outside the logical subspace. We designed a back-gated Si/SiGe double quantum well for spatial mapping of valley splitting with a scanning gate microscope. The lower well serves as an electron reservoir while the upper well hosts a tip-induced quantum dot, and a resonator integrated in the tip couples to dot transitions for pulsed-gate spectroscopy of the valley splitting. Inter-well spacing, well depth, and well width are optimized for sufficient tunnel coupling between dot and reservoir. Earlier simulations showed that a tip-induced dot formed near source and drain electrodes can be moved adiabatically far from the gates, so that changes in valley splitting across an interface defect can be detected. The platform allows mapping before gate deposition and deterministic placement of qubits where the splitting is large.

Charge-noise sensing with valley states

Charge noise is the dominant error source in semiconductor spin qubits, but in situ characterization is difficult because measuring the logical state induces collapse. This project introduces a non-demolition probe that reads out the valley degree of freedom: interface steps and alloy disorder give valley states a transition dipole that couples to an on-chip microwave resonator, so valley-splitting fluctuations can be tracked dispersively without disturbing the spin state. Analytical signal-to-noise calculations and tight-binding simulations confirm sub-millisecond measurement times at unity signal-to-noise. We extended this to two-electron singlet-triplet qubits by initializing a spectator electron in an excited valley state to continuously monitor exchange fluctuations during logic gates, enabling real-time recalibration and feedforward correction. We are now examining whether the same approach applies to three-electron exchange-only qubits, which have become prominent in large-scale devices.

Device-level design and validation of pO and SAGE qubits.

p-Orbital (pO) qubits

The p-orbital (pO) qubit encodes quantum information in the orbital degree of freedom of a five-electron silicon dot, controlled by tuning the anisotropy of the confinement potential. Three projects test the idea from the device level up.

Device simulation of pO qubits on a realistic architecture

We simulated a three-dot linear array (Intel Tunnel Falls) using coupled Schrödinger-Poisson electrostatics with full configuration interaction, and compared the results against detuning-axis pulsed spectroscopy (DAPS) measurements from the Nichol group. The single-electron comparison validates valley and orbital energies, while the five-electron spectra show strong agreement, quantifying how gate voltages govern dot anisotropy and orbital splitting to guide pO qubit design.

FCI validation of the five-electron pO qubit

The pO qubit was conceived in a non-interacting shell-filling picture in which four electrons fill the s orbital and the fifth occupies the p manifold, insulating the logical states from electric-dipole charge noise. Using FCI across a range of valley splittings we asked whether this picture survives the Coulomb interaction. Interacting eigenstates spread into higher orbital shells, but exact symmetries of the Hamiltonian (rotation, in-plane reflection, parity) guarantee degenerate ground states with zero dipole moment. Above a valley-splitting crossover near 50 μeV the quadrupole coupling needed for control is preserved, validating pO operation in high-valley-splitting Si/SiGe devices.

Hole-based pO qubits in Ge and Si

Holes need only three carriers instead of five to reach the p-like manifold, offer enhanced tunability through strong spin-orbit coupling, and have no sample-dependent valley-splitting randomness. Using FCI with analytical confinement potentials we assess whether hole pO qubits in strained Ge and Si quantum wells retain dipole-free noise protection while delivering faster and more uniform control.

With John Nichol and Jiuyang Duan (University of Rochester), and Joseph Caporaletti and J. P. Kestner (University of Maryland, Baltimore County)

Device design for SAGE qubits

Singlet-only always-on gapless exchange (SAGE) qubits, proposed by the Das Sarma group at Maryland (Foulk et al., PRL 2025), encode one qubit in the spins of four electrons in four tunnel-coupled quantum dots. The singlet-only encoding is invariant under magnetic field gradients, so the qubit is protected from errors caused by nuclear spins and g-factor variations, and the always-on exchange couplings suppress leakage energetically, while raising the sensitivity to charge noise. We use MaSQE to design realistic Si/SiGe gate layouts that realize the required always-on exchange couplings and to evaluate their charge-noise sensitivity from the simulated electronic structure.     Strain, fields, and g-factors in planar Ge.

    Hole qubits in germanium

    The g-factor governs the behavior of semiconductor spin qubits, since it sets the qubit frequency and its sensitivity to electrical and magnetic noise. Experiments in germanium quantum dots have revealed large g-factor variations under small gate voltage changes, indicating strong coupling between electrostatics and spin properties. We give a quantitative explanation based on strain-induced g-tensor modulation. Finite-element simulations of inhomogeneous strain combined with quantum calculations of hole wavefunctions show that device-induced strain produces spatially varying g-tensors, and gate voltages shift the dot within this landscape. Earlier work with Max Russ at QuTech cross-validated our germanium hole physics codes and modeled planar Ge hole qubits in electric and magnetic fields.

    Open and ongoing projects

    Threads that are under way or waiting for someone to pick them up. Contact the people listed if you are interested.

    • A new valley-exchange Hamiltonian from intervalley tunnel coupling

      Electron exchange in double quantum dots is usually modeled as a scalar Heisenberg interaction J, but intervalley tunnel coupling in silicon allows valley flips during hopping. A Schrieffer-Wolff transformation of a two-site Fermi-Hubbard model with all six doubly occupied states gives an effective Hamiltonian with three exchange channels (ordinary hopping, intervalley hopping, and their interference), so exchange strength is valley-configuration dependent and can be fully suppressed when the couplings align. A spin-valley cross term lets ordinary exchange pulses rotate or flip valley states, a new mechanism for valley manipulation and phase measurement. Builds on D. J. King et al., arXiv:2607.09638 (2026).

      David Kanaar (working manuscript)
    • Spin-orbit coupling in silicon from a four-band effective-mass model

      Device-scale simulations for silicon spin qubits usually use a single-band effective-mass model that omits spin-orbit coupling. As fidelities advance, accounting for spin-orbit coupling alongside valley splitting becomes essential for predicting performance limits. This project extends the C++ codebase with a four-band effective-mass model using generic multiband solvers to evaluate state sensitivity to realistic noise, aiming toward self-consistent four-band Schrödinger-Poisson modeling.

      David Kanaar and Adrian Culver (open project)
    • A valley-splitting probe for the three-electron exchange-only qubit

      Non-demolition valley reflectometry has been developed for Loss-DiVincenzo and singlet-triplet qubits, but its application to three-electron exchange-only qubits is unexplored. This project formulates a three-electron Hamiltonian with an excited valley state and models how charge noise maps into the dispersive response of a coupled resonator, an in situ diagnostic for all-electrical qubit architectures.

      David Kanaar and Joshua White (in progress)
    • Predicting alloy disorder from valley-splitting maps with neural networks

      Atomic-scale alloy disorder at the Si/SiGe interface cannot be measured directly in situ, but spatially resolved valley-splitting landscapes can be mapped experimentally. This project trains neural networks to invert the modeling pipeline, inferring disorder characteristics from experimental valley-splitting maps as a materials-screening diagnostic.

      David Kanaar and Ron Nodel (open project)
    • Tie-bar region of charge stability diagrams

      Extension of the digital-twin work to accurately model non-constant lever arms and tunnel couplings near the (1,0)/(0,1) boundary during Pauli-blockade readout.

      David Kanaar and Ron Nodel
    • Q3D versus Q1dxClassical2d benchmark

      Comparative performance analysis of two device-modeling frameworks within MaSQE.

      Ron Nodel and David Kanaar
