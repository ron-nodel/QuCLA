MaSQE (Modeling and Simulation for Quantum Exploration) is our device-modeling suite for gate-defined semiconductor quantum dots. It solves the multi-electron Schrödinger equation by full configuration interaction, self-consistently with the electrostatic potential of a realistic device geometry, on real-space grids across multiple domains and models (MDMM). The result is the energy spectrum of coupled quantum-dot systems in silicon and germanium, accurate enough to guide device design, extract effective parameters such as charging energies, lever arms, and exchange couplings, and compare directly with experiment.

From Nodel et al. 2026, Fig. 1

The first half of MaSQE is the electrostatics. Its multi-domain, multi-model (MDMM) framework takes a gate layout and a layered material stack, meshes the device by domain, and solves the Poisson equation self-consistently with the confined single-particle states, so that the potential in the quantum well reflects the charge that actually sits there. The result is the confinement landscape of a realistic device: lever arms, dot positions, tunnel barriers, and how all of them move with gate voltage.

From Anderson et al. 2022, Fig. 2

The second half is the many-body solver. Starting from the MDMM potential, MaSQE solves the multi-electron Schrödinger equation by full configuration interaction on real-space grids, with a continuously adapted orthonormal orbital basis that gives high-precision ground and excited few-electron states. From these come the energy spectra of coupled quantum dots: charging energies, exchange couplings, orbital and valley splittings, and the charge densities of each state. The code was developed with HRL Laboratories and has been used for exchange-only triple-dot qubits, multielectron dots, and the five-electron pO qubit.

From Nodel et al. 2026, Fig. 3

Operating spin qubits requires tuning the electron occupancy under each gate, traditionally mapped with charge stability diagrams of occupancy versus two gate voltages. Simulating these pixel by pixel from full device models is accurate but computationally prohibitive for design iteration. Using the MDMM framework, an automated tuning procedure identifies a small set of gate-voltage reference points corresponding to specific charge states, and FCI calculations at those points give charging energies, lever arms, and interdot Coulomb interactions that parameterize a Hubbard model directly. The Hubbard model then reconstructs the full diagram efficiently, enabling rapid pre-fabrication optimization of gate geometry and voltage ranges. The method is validated against bias-sweep simulations and experimental data from an Intel Tunnel Falls Si/SiGe device. The framework also identifies experimentally accessible Hubbard parameters for analog quantum simulation.

Extension of the digital-twin work to accurately model non-constant lever arms and tunnel couplings near the (1,0)/(0,1) boundary during Pauli-blockade readout.

Neural-network variational Monte Carlo (VMCNN) represents the many-electron wavefunction with a neural network and optimizes it stochastically, which lets it reach more electrons and larger orbital spaces than full configuration interaction can. With Liang Fu's group at MIT, we use VMCNN to compute exchange couplings in coupled quantum dots and benchmark them against MaSQE FCI on the same device potentials. The goal is to establish where the neural-network approach matches FCI accuracy, so it can take over for the larger, many-electron problems that FCI cannot handle.

With Liang Fu (Massachusetts Institute of Technology)

Charge noise in spin qubit devices is often dominated by individual two-level fluctuators (TLFs), charge traps that switch between two states and shift the dot energies. Fundamental limits on gate fidelities are likely to sit at the materials and device level, so knowing where these fluctuators are is a first step toward removing them. Combining high-precision simulations of realistic devices with experimental data, we developed a procedure that triangulates the spatial location of individual charge fluctuators from their signatures on different gates. From Kanaar et al. 2025, Fig. 1

Charge noise is the dominant error source in semiconductor spin qubits, but in situ characterization is difficult because measuring the logical state induces collapse. This project introduces a non-demolition probe that reads out the valley degree of freedom: interface steps and alloy disorder give valley states a transition dipole that couples to an on-chip microwave resonator, so valley-splitting fluctuations can be tracked dispersively without disturbing the spin state. Analytical signal-to-noise calculations and tight-binding simulations confirm sub-millisecond measurement times at unity signal-to-noise. We extended this to two-electron singlet-triplet qubits by initializing a spectator electron in an excited valley state to continuously monitor exchange fluctuations during logic gates, enabling real-time recalibration and feedforward correction.

Non-demolition valley reflectometry has been developed for Loss-DiVincenzo and singlet-triplet qubits, but its application to three-electron exchange-only qubits is unexplored. This project formulates a three-electron Hamiltonian with an excited valley state and models how charge noise maps into the dispersive response of a coupled resonator, an in situ diagnostic for all-electrical qubit architectures.

Radiation passing through a device creates electron-hole pairs that can migrate into the active region, change the occupancy of charge traps, and reconfigure two-level fluctuators. The resulting change in the electrostatic environment shifts individual dot energies, tunnel couplings, and the exchange between dots. Using MaSQE, we place charge monopoles on a fine grid around a three-dot Si/SiGe device (P1, P2, P3) and resolve sub-nanometer charge displacements accurately, mapping how much each dot's chemical potential and the inter-dot detuning move in response. Filling a single trap in the active region shifts dot energies and detuning on the order of meV, enough to move an exchange-only qubit off its symmetric operating point. The autotuning code now accounts for these charges, so we can compute the gate-voltage corrections needed to restore the qubits after a radiation event.

We place point-source charges directly in the device and track how exchange shifts, including charges right at the interface, half a nanometer to a nanometer below the oxide. The aim is to find how close a charge can sit to the interface before it causes problems for the qubit, and how far gate voltages must move to bring an exchange-only qubit back to its symmetric operating point.

Which local potentials at the interface trap an electron or hole, how deep those traps are, and how others in the field model them are open questions. A trap model sets how much of the transported charge sticks. Given a flux of particles arriving at the interface, it decides which charges stay, and the trapped charges and their positions then become the input to the device simulation. Potentials informed by density functional theory are one route.

Earlier work by Leah Tom mapped how two-level fluctuators shift detuning, lever arms, and chemical potentials. The next question is the effect of charge noise on exchange itself: assuming a distribution of fluctuators in the device, each able to move by about a dipole length, what distribution of exchange energies results.

From Kanaar et al. 2026, Fig. 2

Electrons in strained silicon quantum wells carry a low-lying valley degeneracy whose splitting is set by atomic-scale alloy disorder at the Si/SiGe interface. Small or random valley splittings are therefore a primary obstacle to reliable spin-qubit operation, initialization, and readout. Using one-dimensional tight-binding device-scale simulations (2k0 theory) that incorporate atomistic alloy disorder, we identified a stack, a narrow quantum well combined with a small Ge spike and a pure-Ge cap, that consistently yields valley splittings of 1 to 5 meV across disorder realizations, corroborated by density functional theory. The germanium-spike idea builds on earlier experimental work by group alumni.

From Cakar et al. 2024, Fig. 3

Spatial variations in valley splitting are a challenge for high-fidelity Si/SiGe quantum dot qubits, because low-lying valley states lead to leakage outside the logical subspace. We designed a back-gated Si/SiGe double quantum well for spatial mapping of valley splitting with a scanning gate microscope. The lower well serves as an electron reservoir while the upper well hosts a tip-induced quantum dot, and a resonator integrated in the tip couples to dot transitions for pulsed-gate spectroscopy of the valley splitting. Inter-well spacing, well depth, and well width are optimized for sufficient tunnel coupling between dot and reservoir. Earlier simulations showed that a tip-induced dot formed near source and drain electrodes can be moved adiabatically far from the gates, so that changes in valley splitting across an interface defect can be detected. The platform allows mapping before gate deposition and deterministic placement of qubits where the splitting is large.

Electron exchange in double quantum dots is usually modeled as a scalar Heisenberg interaction J, but intervalley tunnel coupling in silicon allows valley flips during hopping. A Schrieffer-Wolff transformation of a two-site Fermi-Hubbard model with all six doubly occupied states gives an effective Hamiltonian with three exchange channels (ordinary hopping, intervalley hopping, and their interference), so exchange strength is valley-configuration dependent and can be fully suppressed when the couplings align. A spin-valley cross term lets ordinary exchange pulses rotate or flip valley states, a new mechanism for valley manipulation and phase measurement. Builds on D. J. King et al., arXiv:2607.09638 (2026). Working manuscript.

The p-orbital (pO) qubit encodes quantum information in the orbital degree of freedom of a five-electron silicon dot, controlled by tuning the anisotropy of the confinement potential. Three projects test the idea from the device level up.

We simulated a three-dot linear array (Intel Tunnel Falls) using coupled Schrödinger-Poisson electrostatics with full configuration interaction, and compared the results against detuning-axis pulsed spectroscopy (DAPS) measurements from the Nichol group. The single-electron comparison validates valley and orbital energies, while the five-electron spectra show strong agreement, quantifying how gate voltages govern dot anisotropy and orbital splitting to guide pO qubit design.

The pO qubit was conceived in a non-interacting shell-filling picture in which four electrons fill the s orbital and the fifth occupies the p manifold, insulating the logical states from electric-dipole charge noise. Using FCI across a range of valley splittings we asked whether this picture survives the Coulomb interaction. Interacting eigenstates spread into higher orbital shells, but exact symmetries of the Hamiltonian (rotation, in-plane reflection, parity) guarantee degenerate ground states with zero dipole moment. Above a valley-splitting crossover near 50 μeV the quadrupole coupling needed for control is preserved, validating pO operation in high-valley-splitting Si/SiGe devices.

Holes need only three carriers instead of five to reach the p-like manifold, offer enhanced tunability through strong spin-orbit coupling, and have no sample-dependent valley-splitting randomness. Using FCI with analytical confinement potentials we assess whether hole pO qubits in strained Ge and Si quantum wells retain dipole-free noise protection while delivering faster and more uniform control.

Singlet-only always-on gapless exchange (SAGE) qubits, proposed by the Das Sarma group at Maryland (Foulk et al., PRL 2025), encode one qubit in the spins of four electrons in four tunnel-coupled quantum dots. The singlet-only encoding is invariant under magnetic field gradients, so the qubit is protected from errors caused by nuclear spins and g-factor variations, and the always-on exchange couplings suppress leakage energetically, while raising the sensitivity to charge noise. We use MaSQE to design realistic Si/SiGe gate layouts that realize the required always-on exchange couplings and to evaluate their charge-noise sensitivity from the simulated electronic structure. From Niu et al. 2026, Fig. 10

The g-factor governs the behavior of semiconductor spin qubits, since it sets the qubit frequency and its sensitivity to electrical and magnetic noise. Experiments in germanium quantum dots have revealed large g-factor variations under small gate voltage changes, indicating strong coupling between electrostatics and spin properties. We give a quantitative explanation based on strain-induced g-tensor modulation. Finite-element simulations of inhomogeneous strain combined with quantum calculations of hole wavefunctions show that device-induced strain produces spatially varying g-tensors, and gate voltages shift the dot within this landscape. Earlier work with Max Russ at QuTech cross-validated our germanium hole physics codes and modeled planar Ge hole qubits in electric and magnetic fields.

Open projects

Contact the people listed if you are interested.

  • Device-scale simulations for silicon spin qubits usually use a single-band effective-mass model that omits spin-orbit coupling. As fidelities advance, accounting for spin-orbit coupling alongside valley splitting becomes essential for predicting performance limits. This project extends the C++ codebase with a four-band effective-mass model using generic multiband solvers to evaluate state sensitivity to realistic noise, aiming toward self-consistent four-band Schrödinger-Poisson modeling.

    David Kanaar and Adrian Culver

  • Atomic-scale alloy disorder at the Si/SiGe interface cannot be measured directly in situ, but spatially resolved valley-splitting landscapes can be mapped experimentally. This project trains neural networks to invert the modeling pipeline, inferring disorder characteristics from experimental valley-splitting maps as a materials-screening diagnostic.

    David Kanaar and Ron Nodel

  • Model the potential a scanning-gate tip induces in the device and how a nearby charge fluctuator changes it, to see whether a scanning probe can pick out and locate individual fluctuators. Compare this against conveyor-mode shuttling, where a dot carried along a channel samples the local potential, as a second way to detect fluctuators. Related: Tom et al. 2025, which uses electrostatic force microscopy to find charge-switching defects in aluminum oxide.

    Ron Nodel (lower priority)
